## Satellite-Based Coastal Change Detection and Shoreline Monitoring

Coastal erosion is an important environmental concern because it can result in the loss of coastal land, damage to infrastructure, changes in coastal ecosystems, and increased exposure of nearby communities to coastal hazards. Monitoring shoreline changes is therefore necessary for understanding how coastlines evolve and for supporting coastal management and planning. Traditional shoreline surveys can provide detailed information about coastal conditions, but conducting repeated field surveys over large areas can require considerable time and resources. Remote sensing provides an alternative by allowing researchers to observe coastal environments through satellite images acquired at different times. When combined with Geographic Information Systems (GIS), satellite imagery can be used to identify shoreline positions, compare their locations across different dates, and estimate the extent and rate of coastal change.

One of the important developments in remote sensing for identifying water features was introduced by McFeeters (1996), who proposed the Normalized Difference Water Index (NDWI). The index was developed to enhance open-water features in remotely sensed images and distinguish them from surrounding land and vegetation. Although the index was initially introduced for identifying water bodies rather than specifically monitoring coastal erosion, its ability to distinguish water from surrounding land makes it useful for shoreline extraction. In the current project, NDWI will be applied to Sentinel-2 imagery using the green band (Band 3) and near-infrared band (Band 8) to identify the boundary between land and water across the selected Alexandria coastal sectors. The extracted water–land boundaries will then provide the basis for comparing shoreline positions across different years (McFeeters, 1996).

## Remote Sensing Applications in Egyptian Coastal Areas

The application of satellite imagery and GIS techniques to coastal monitoring has been demonstrated in several studies conducted along the Egyptian Mediterranean coast. Abou Samra and Ali (2021) investigated shoreline changes along the Nile Delta coastline using Landsat satellite imagery and the Digital Shoreline Analysis System (DSAS). Their study used twelve Landsat scenes covering the period from 1984 to 2018. After extracting shoreline positions from the satellite images, the researchers applied DSAS to calculate shoreline displacement rates and identify areas experiencing erosion, accretion, or relative stability. Their results indicated that approximately 44.2% of the studied coastline experienced erosion, while 45% experienced accretion and 10.8% remained stable during the study period. The researchers also found higher erosion rates around the Rosetta and Damietta promontories compared with other sections of the Nile Delta coastline. These findings demonstrate that coastal change is not uniform across the region and that different coastal sections may experience different patterns of shoreline movement. The study is relevant to the current project because it provides an example of using multi-temporal satellite imagery and spatial analysis to monitor coastal changes in Egypt. However, its geographical coverage and use of Landsat imagery differ from the current project, which focuses on three selected coastal sectors in Alexandria and uses Sentinel-2 imagery to investigate more recent shoreline changes between 2018 and 2026 (Abou Samra & Ali, 2021).

The development of the Sentinel-2 satellite mission has provided additional opportunities for coastal monitoring through freely available multispectral imagery. Sentinel-2 is part of the European Union's Copernicus programme and carries a Multispectral Instrument (MSI) that records information across thirteen spectral bands. Four of these bands have a spatial resolution of 10 metres, including the green band (Band 3) and near-infrared band (Band 8), which are particularly relevant to water-boundary identification using NDWI. The Sentinel-2 constellation also provides a nominal five-day revisit time, allowing researchers to select images from different dates and observe changes in land and water surfaces (European Space Agency [ESA], n.d.). The availability of these images makes Sentinel-2 a suitable data source for projects that require repeated observations without depending entirely on commercial satellite imagery. In the current project, its multispectral data will provide a consistent source for extracting and comparing shoreline positions across the selected Alexandria sectors. Nevertheless, the 10-metre spatial resolution of the bands used in the analysis places limitations on the level of detail that can be detected, particularly when shoreline changes are relatively small. Therefore, the extracted shoreline positions should be interpreted with consideration of the spatial resolution and other possible sources of measurement error (ESA, n.d.).

## Accuracy of Sentinel-2 Shoreline Extraction

As satellite-derived shoreline extraction became more widely used, researchers increasingly examined not only how shorelines could be extracted but also how accurately the resulting lines represented the actual coastal boundary. Pucino et al. (2022) investigated the accuracy of instantaneous sub-pixel shorelines extracted from Sentinel-2 imagery by comparing them with ground-truth data collected through Unmanned Aerial Vehicle (UAV) surveys. Their research included 47 UAV surveys conducted during Sentinel-2 overpasses across 15 sites over a period of three years. The researchers tested different shoreline extraction methods, image thresholding techniques, and water-level estimation approaches. One of their findings was that the best-performing shoreline extractor generally achieved its best performance along only 30–40% of a beach. This indicates that the performance of a particular extraction method can vary considerably even within the same coastal environment. The study therefore highlights the importance of considering local coastal characteristics and validating extracted shorelines rather than assuming that one method will perform equally well across all locations. This is particularly relevant to the current project because the three selected Alexandria sectors may differ in their coastal structures, surface characteristics, and surrounding land uses. Consequently, the results of NDWI-based extraction should be inspected and assessed for each sector instead of treating all extracted shorelines as equally accurate (Pucino et al., 2022).

A further contribution was made by Vos et al. (2023), who presented a benchmarking framework for evaluating satellite-derived shoreline mapping algorithms. Their study compared five established algorithms using publicly available Landsat and Sentinel-2 imagery and evaluated their performance at four sandy beaches with different geological and oceanographic conditions. The extracted shorelines were compared against long-term in-situ beach surveys to assess their horizontal accuracy. The results showed that the tested algorithms generally achieved a horizontal accuracy of approximately 10 metres at microtidal sites. However, accuracy decreased at locations with larger tidal ranges and more complex coastal conditions, exceeding 20 metres at one high-energy macrotidal beach in France. The authors also discussed several factors that can introduce errors into satellite-derived shoreline measurements, including image georeferencing, spatial resolution, water-level variations, and the shoreline detection method. Their findings demonstrate that the quality of shoreline extraction depends not only on the selected algorithm but also on the characteristics of the study area and the quality of the input imagery. This is important for the current project because it supports the need for consistent image selection, careful preprocessing, and accuracy assessment when comparing shoreline positions across different years. It also suggests that apparent changes in shoreline position should be interpreted with consideration of possible measurement errors, particularly when the measured displacement is relatively small (Vos et al., 2023).

In another study published in 2024, Pardo-Pascual et al. (2024) evaluated satellite-derived shorelines automatically extracted from Sentinel-2 imagery using the Satellite-derived Shoreline Extraction Tool (SAET). The researchers compared the extracted shorelines with reference shorelines obtained from nearly coincident very-high-resolution satellite images. Their evaluation covered nine test sites with different morphological and oceanographic conditions along the Atlantic European and western Mediterranean coasts. The results showed that shoreline extraction errors varied according to coastal typology, with RMSE values ranging from 3.7 to 13.5 metres when appropriate extraction parameters were selected. The researchers also evaluated different image-processing levels and water-segmentation methods. Their findings indicated that the Automated Water Extraction Index for images without shadows (AWEInsh), using a zero threshold, generally performed best for the initial water segmentation. They also found that morphological operations, such as dilation and erosion, could improve the results for certain coastal types, while atmospheric correction had a smaller overall influence on shoreline accuracy. These findings are relevant to the proposed project because they demonstrate that the accuracy of automated shoreline extraction depends on the physical characteristics of the coastline and the processing methods used. They also provide a useful reference for understanding the possible accuracy range of Sentinel-2-derived shorelines. However, the reported RMSE values belong to the specific study sites and methods used by Pardo-Pascual et al. (2024); they should not be assumed to represent the expected accuracy of the current project without local validation.

## Remote Sensing and Coastal Change Along the Egyptian Mediterranean Coast

Research conducted specifically along the Egyptian Mediterranean coast also demonstrates the value of multi-temporal satellite imagery for understanding environmental changes associated with coastal development. El-Masry et al. (2024) investigated multi-decadal land-use and land-cover changes in the area between El-Dabaa and Ras El-Hekma along the Mediterranean coast of Egypt. Their study used Landsat satellite images from 1990, 2010, and 2020 to examine changes in land-use and land-cover patterns over a thirty-year period. The researchers applied supervised classification and post-classification comparison to investigate changes in agricultural land, bare soil, and built-up areas. Their results showed an expansion of agricultural land by approximately 47.33 km², representing 4.13%, while the bare-soil area decreased by approximately 73.13 km², representing 6.24%. The study also reported an increase in built-up areas associated with tourism and urban development. The researchers emphasized the importance of monitoring land-use changes in coastal areas experiencing development and highlighted how remote sensing can provide information that supports land management and planning. Although this study focused on land-use and land-cover changes rather than directly measuring shoreline displacement, it is relevant to the current project because it demonstrates the application of multi-temporal satellite imagery to monitor environmental changes along the Egyptian Mediterranean coast. It also supports the broader use of spatial data analysis to understand coastal environments and provide information that can assist decision-makers (El-Masry et al., 2024).

## Research Gap and Project Contribution

The reviewed literature demonstrates the growing importance of satellite imagery and GIS-based methods in coastal monitoring. Earlier work established the use of spectral indices such as NDWI for distinguishing water from land (McFeeters, 1996), while subsequent research applied satellite imagery and spatial analysis to measure shoreline changes along the Egyptian coast (Abou Samra & Ali, 2021). More recent studies have focused on the accuracy of satellite-derived shorelines and have shown that extraction performance can vary according to image resolution, coastal characteristics, water levels, and the methods used (Pucino et al., 2022; Vos et al., 2023; Pardo-Pascual et al., 2024). Research along the Egyptian Mediterranean coast has also demonstrated the usefulness of multi-temporal satellite data for monitoring broader environmental and land-use changes (El-Masry et al., 2024).

Despite these contributions, the reviewed studies do not provide the same combination of methods and geographical coverage proposed in this project. In particular, the current project focuses on three selected coastal sectors in Alexandria: Corniche Infill, Eastern Harbour, and Montaza Bay. It will use Sentinel-2 imagery to examine shoreline positions over the period from 2018 to 2026, applying a consistent NDWI-based extraction approach and comparing the resulting shoreline positions across the selected years. The extracted shorelines will then be used to calculate shoreline displacement and annualized rates of change, allowing the project to identify areas showing possible erosion, accretion, or relative stability. In addition to the geospatial analysis, the project will organize the resulting data in a PostGIS database and present the findings through an interactive dashboard. This will bring together image-based shoreline extraction, spatial analysis, data management, and visualization in one workflow.

An important consideration is that the project uses one selected satellite image per year rather than continuous or seasonal observations. Therefore, the results will represent shoreline positions at the dates of the selected images and provide an indication of changes between those observations. They will not capture all seasonal shoreline fluctuations or short-term coastal events. Moreover, the accuracy of the extracted shoreline positions will depend on image quality, coastal conditions, and the extraction process. These limitations will be considered when interpreting the calculated shoreline changes and presenting the results. Overall, the project aims to provide a practical and organized approach to monitoring shoreline change across the selected Alexandria coastal sectors and to make the resulting spatial information accessible through an interactive mapping platform.
